    For stronger players

    1...b6 prepares ...Bb7 to hit e4 from far away. It is easy to learn and avoids theory, but it hands White the center, and if Black is slow to challenge it White gets an easy space advantage.

    Below 1200 three in ten answer 2...Bb7 with 3.d5, which blocks the bishop but leaves the pawn loose. At 2000+ half play 3.Bd3. White scores about 55% after 3.Bd3, so the Owen is a surprise weapon rather than a main repertoire.

    Traps and how to punish them

    3...f5?! 4.exf5 Bxg2? 5.Qh5+

    Grabbing the g2 pawn spends the move Black needed for ...Nf6, so 5.Qh5+ comes down the open e8–h5 diagonal. After 5.Qh5+ g6 6.fxg6 the natural 6...Nf6?? loses at once: 7.gxh7+ is a discovered check, and after 7...Nxh5 8.Bg6 is mate.

    Fewer than 20 games in our data reached the end of this line.

    Show on the board: 1.e4 b6 2.d4 Bb7 3.Bd3 f5 4.exf5 Bxg2 5.Qh5+ g6 6.fxg6 Nf6 7.gxh7+ Nxh5 8.Bg6#(ends in mate)

    What now: plans for both sides

    White

    • 2.d4 Bb7 3.Bd3 is the most common setup at 2000+: the bishop protects e4 and points at the kingside.
    • 3.Nc3 also protects e4; follow up with Nf3, O-O and Re1.
    • Do not hurry with e5 or d5; a solid center backed by c3 or c4 is enough.

    Black

    • After 3.Bd3 play 3...e6 with ...c5 or ...Nf6: hit the center before White finishes developing.
    • The b7 bishop is your best piece, so keep the long diagonal open.
    • If White plays 3.d5, attack the pawn at once with ...e6 or ...Nf6.
