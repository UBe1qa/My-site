Plans for both sides

White

  • Castle, play Re1 and c3, then d4. The bishop usually retreats Ba4–b3 and aims at f7.
  • The pressure on e5 is indirect: Bxc6 followed by Nxe5 only works once e5 has lost a defender.
  • In closed lines the knight goes Nbd2–f1–g3 and White attacks on the kingside.

Black

  • 3...a6 asks the bishop to decide straight away; 3...Nf6 (the Berlin) hits e4.
  • After ...b5 and ...Bb7 or ...d6, Black has a solid wall and queenside space.
  • Do not rush ...d5 before your king is safe; e5 holds if you keep ...d6 and ...Nc6.

Common traps

Exchange Variation: 5.Nxe5? Qd4

After 4.Bxc6 dxc6 the e5 pawn looks free, but 5.Nxe5? Qd4! attacks the knight and e4 at once, and Black wins the pawn back with a good game.

In our data, the last move of this line was played in 2,053 games.

Show on the board: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 4.Bxc6 dxc6 5.Nxe5 Qd4 6.Nf3 Qxe4+

Noah’s Ark trap

If White takes the pawn back with 8.Qxd4?, Black plays ...c5 and ...c4, and the bishop on b3 has no way out. To avoid it, prepare d4 with 5.c3 instead of playing 5.d4 at once.

Fewer than 6 games in our data reached the end of this line.

At club level

Below 1600 the Exchange Variation (4.Bxc6) is White’s most common choice, and many Black players panic about e5. You do not need to: after 4...dxc6 5.Nxe5? Qd4 wins the pawn back. For White, the closed main lines need a plan more than memory: c3, d4, Nbd2–f1–g3.
