1.d4 Nf6 Indian Defenses: Every Family and Stats by Rating

1...Nf6 stops e4 without committing a pawn, so Black can still choose almost any structure. After 2.c4, Black’s second move picks the family: ...g6 for the King’s Indian or Grünfeld, ...e6 for the Nimzo, Queen’s Indian or Bogo, ...c5 for the Benoni and Benko, ...e5 for the Budapest. White can also skip c4 with the London, the Torre or the Trompowsky.

Plans for both sides

White

  • 2.c4 is the main move: close to half the games at 2000+, but under one in five below 1200, where 2.Bf4 (the London) is played most.
  • 2.Nf3 keeps options open and avoids the Budapest and the Benko; 2.Bg5 is the Trompowsky, and 2.Nf3 with 3.Bg5 the Torre Attack.
  • Against ...g6 lines, decide early whether you want a big center (Nc3 and e4) or a quiet fianchetto (g3 and Bg2).

Black

  • Pick one structure and learn it well: ...e6 with ...d5 or ...Bb4 is solid, ...g6 with ...d6 is combative.
  • Against 2.Nf3, 2...g6 (the East Indian), 2...d5 and 2...e6 are all common and let you keep your own repertoire.
  • Against an early Bg5 (Torre or Trompowsky), ...Ne4, or ...e6 with ...h6, asks the bishop what it wants.

At club level

Below 1200, 2.Bf4 is the most common answer to 1...Nf6 (about one game in four), and after 2.c4 Black chooses 2...g6 most often, then 2...d5 and 2...e6. At 2000+ 2.c4 is played in close to half the games, and 2...e6 becomes the top reply at about 41%. White scores about 51% after 1...Nf6 at every level.
