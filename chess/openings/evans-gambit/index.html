    For stronger players

    4.b4 offers a pawn to pull the c5 bishop away. If Black takes, White plays c3 and d4 with gain of time and aims every piece at f7 and the black king. Kasparov brought it back to top level in the 1990s, and at 2000+ almost nine in ten of our players take the pawn.

    Below 1200 about four in five players answer 5.c3 with 5...Bc5, which walks into d4 with tempo. At 2000+ half play 5...Ba5 and about a quarter 5...Be7. If you play the Evans as White, prepare 6.d4 against all three.

    11. Opens the center while Black is behind in development.

    12. Takes another pawn.

    13. Castles and gives even more for a fast attack.

    What now: plans for both sides

    White

    • After 4...Bxb4 5.c3 the bishop must move again, and d4 follows with tempo. Castle fast and do not hurry to win the pawn back.
    • Against 5...Ba5 the main line is 6.d4; 6.O-O and 6.Qb3 are good alternatives with the same idea: open the center while Black’s king is still at home.
    • If Black declines with 4...Bb6, 5.a4 gains queenside space and makes Black spend time on ...a6 or ...a5.

    Black

    • Taking with 4...Bxb4 is the critical test. After 5.c3 the safest retreats are 5...Ba5 and 5...Be7.
    • 5...Ba5 6.d4 d6 7.O-O Bb6 is Lasker’s Defense: Black returns the pawn, trades queens and reaches a level endgame.
    • Put king safety first: castle quickly and trade queens when you can, since every exchange takes steam out of the attack.

    Variations

    • Black holds the center with ...d6; 7.Qb3 hits f7 and b7 at once.

    • Black returns the pawn to trade queens. The endgame is about level and the attack is gone, which is why many gambit players avoid 7.O-O here.
