Modern and Old Benoni: Lines, Plans and Stats by Rating

The Benoni gives White a space advantage on purpose. After 3.d5 e6 4.Nc3 exd5 5.cxd5 d6 Black has a queenside pawn majority and a fianchettoed bishop, while White has the central majority and plays for e4–e5. Both sides know what they want, which makes for sharp games.

Plans for both sides

White

  • e4, Nf3, Be2 and O-O is the Classical setup; a4 and Nd2–c4 then hold back ...b5.
  • f4 with Bb5+ (the Taimanov line) aims for a fast e5 and is the most dangerous try.
  • The e5 break is the main plan, and the d6 pawn is a long-term target.

Black

  • ...g6, ...Bg7, ...O-O and ...Re8 put pressure on e4.
  • The ...b5 break is your counterplay: prepare it with ...a6 and ...Nbd7 or ...Rb8.
  • Against 1.d4 c5 2.d5, the Old Benoni with ...d6 and ...e5 gives a slower, closed game.

At club level

After 2...c5 3.d5, about half the players at 1600 and up choose 3...b5 (the Benko) and about a quarter 3...e6 (the Modern Benoni). Against 1.d4 c5, players below 1200 take on c5 far more often than they push d5 (37% against 13%), and at 1600 to 1999 Black does well after 2.dxc5, with White at only about 48%.
