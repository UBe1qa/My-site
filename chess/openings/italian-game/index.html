    For stronger players

    3.Bc4 points the bishop at f7, the one square only Black’s king defends. From here the game goes two ways: a slow build-up with d3 and c3 (the Giuoco Pianissimo), or a sharp fight after 3...Nf6 4.Ng5.

    In club games the Italian is often decided by tactics on f7 and e5 in the first ten moves rather than by deep theory. As Black, know 4.Ng5 d5 5.exd5 Na5 and what to do against both 4.d3 and c3–d4; as White, know the c3–d4 plan and when Ng5 is safe.

    Traps and how to punish them

    The 3...Nd4 trick

    3...Nd4 seems to drop e5, but taking it is the trap. After 4.Nxe5? Qg5 White is already worse, and only 5.O-O or 5.Bxf7+ keeps the damage small. The greedy 5.Nxf7? loses to 5...Qxg2 6.Rf1 Qxe4+ 7.Be2 Nf3 mate. The calm 4.Nxd4 exd4 5.O-O leaves White clearly better.

    What now: plans for both sides

    White

    • Castle early, then choose c3 + d4 for a big center or d3 for a slow, safe game.
    • In the slow lines the knight travels Nbd2–f1–g3 and the a-pawn goes to a4 to stop ...b5.
    • 4.Ng5 only makes sense after 3...Nf6, when the f6 knight blocks the queen’s path to g5. After 3...Bc5, 4.Ng5? Qxg5 just wins the knight.

    Black

    • 3...Bc5 copies White and fights for d4; 3...Nf6 counter-attacks e4 straight away.
    • The freeing break is ...d5. If you get it in safely, you are equal.
    • After 4.Ng5 d5 5.exd5, play 5...Na5. The natural 5...Nxd5 leaves f7 open to 6.Nxf7 or 6.d4.
